1 Executive Summary

In federal and official statistics, the combination of a large non-probability (“convenience”) sample (such as commercial web panels or administrative filings) with a smaller, high-quality probability reference survey offers substantial promise for reducing survey costs and respondent burden while expanding data coverage. However, unweighted convenience data suffer from severe selection bias (Meng, 2018).

This report implements and benchmarks the Latent Binary Bayesian Neural Network (LBBNN) framework for estimating unknown convenience participation propensities \(\pi_{ci} = \mathbb{P}(I_{ci} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i)\) under five composite likelihood and pseudo-likelihood formulations:

  1. STACK: Composite Bernoulli likelihood on the unlinked stacked sample \(S = S_c \oplus S_r\) (\(\pi_{zi} = \frac{\pi_{ci}}{\pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri}}\)).
  2. LINK1: Deterministically linked union sample \(S = S_c \cup (S_r \setminus S_c)\) (\(\pi_{zi} = \frac{\pi_{ci}}{\pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri} - \pi_{ci}\pi_{ri}}\)).
  3. LINK2: Deterministically linked stacked sample \(S = S_c \oplus S_r\) (\(\pi_{zi} = \frac{\pi_{ci}(1 + \pi_{ri})}{\pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri}}\)).
  4. MIP (Missing Information Principle): Probabilistically linked stacked sample with linkage informativeness parameter \(p_{\text{link}} \in [0, 1]\).
  5. PSEUDO: Pseudo-log-likelihood approach of Chen, Li & Wu (2020) utilizing survey design weights \(w_{ri} = 1 / \pi_{ri}\) on \(S_r\).

Through automated variational spike-and-slab edge selection, LBBNN simultaneously: - Captures complex non-linear propensity curves and multi-way interactions without manual polynomial expansions. - Prunes uninformative noise covariates from the network architecture. - Prevents the catastrophic variance and weight explosion that afflicts classical pseudo-likelihood under low common support.

2 Theoretical Foundations

2.1 The Challenge of Non-Probability Convenience Data

Let \(U = \{1, \dots, N\}\) denote a finite target population. Each unit \(i \in U\) is associated with: - A vector of auxiliary covariates \(\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^p\). - An outcome of interest \(y_i \in \mathbb{R}\). - An unobserved convenience sample participation indicator \(I_{ci} \in \{0, 1\}\), with unknown participation propensity \(\pi_{ci} = \mathbb{P}(I_{ci} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) > 0\). - A probability survey inclusion indicator \(I_{ri} \in \{0, 1\}\), with known design selection probability \(\pi_{ri} = \mathbb{P}(I_{ri} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) > 0\).

If units with particular values of \(\mathbf{x}_i\) or \(y_i\) systematically self-select into the convenience dataset, the naive sample mean is severely biased:

\[\text{Bias}(\bar{y}_c) = \frac{\text{Cov}_U(I_c, Y)}{\bar{\pi}_c}\]

As Meng (2018) proved, this selection bias scales with \(\sqrt{N}\) rather than sample size \(n_c\): even a massive convenience dataset comprising 80% or 90% of the population can produce wildly misleading population estimates if \(\text{Corr}(I_c, Y) \neq 0\).

2.2 The Five Estimation Formulations (Beresovsky, Gershunskaya & Savitsky, 2026)

2.2.1 The Fundamental Identification Problem & The Conditioning Trick

In standard supervised machine learning, fitting a binary classification model \(\mathbb{P}(I_{ci} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i)\) requires a training sample of both positive (\(I_{ci} = 1\)) and negative (\(I_{ci} = 0\)) units drawn from the population. In the non-probability survey setting, however, we encounter a fundamental identification barrier: 1. We observe auxiliary covariates \(\mathbf{x}_i\) only for units that participated in the convenience sample \(S_c = \{i \in U : I_{ci} = 1\}\) and units selected into the reference probability survey \(S_r = \{i \in U : I_{ri} = 1\}\). 2. For all non-sampled population units (\(i \notin S_c \cup S_r\)), the covariates \(\mathbf{x}_i\) are entirely unobserved.

Consequently, one cannot directly fit a standard binary regression for \(I_{ci}\) over \(U\). Beresovsky, Gershunskaya & Savitsky (2026) resolve this challenge through a conditioning trick: by combining \(S_c\) and \(S_r\) into a combined sample \(S\) and conditioning on the event that a unit was observed in \(S\), the known reference design probability \(\pi_{ri}\) acts as a structural offset that anchors the neural network’s unconstrained logits \(\eta_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)\) to the true population scale, allowing exact recovery of \(\pi_{ci}(\mathbf{x}_i; \theta) = \sigma(\eta_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i))\).

2.2.2 Unlinked Stacked Sample (STACK)

When records between the convenience and reference files cannot be matched, we form the stacked multiset \(S = S_c \oplus S_r\) of size \(n = n_c + n_r\). Any population unit \(i \in S_c \cap S_r\) that happens to be selected into both files appears twice in \(S\): once in the \(S_c\) block with origin indicator \(D_i = 1\), and once in the \(S_r\) block with \(D_i = 0\).

Defining the target response indicator as \(I_{zi} = D_i\), we evaluate the conditional probability that a record with covariate profile \(\mathbf{x}_i\) observed in \(S_c \oplus S_r\) originated from \(S_c\) using Bayes’ rule:

\[\pi_{zi}^{\text{STACK}} = \mathbb{P}(D_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i, i \in S_c \oplus S_r) = \frac{\mathbb{E}[I_{ci} \mid \mathbf{x}_i]}{\mathbb{E}[I_{ci} + I_{ri} \mid \mathbf{x}_i]} = \frac{\pi_{ci}}{\pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri}}\]

Writing the convenience propensity as \(\pi_{ci} = \sigma(\eta_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i))\), the log-odds of \(\pi_{zi}^{\text{STACK}}\) satisfy:

\[\log\left(\frac{\pi_{zi}^{\text{STACK}}}{1 - \pi_{zi}^{\text{STACK}}}\right) = \log\pi_{ci} - \log\pi_{ri} = \eta_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i) - \log(1 + e^{\eta_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)}) - \log\pi_{ri}\]

In typical survey settings where \(\pi_{ci} \ll 1\), this behaves as an unconstrained binary classification model where \(-\log\pi_{ri}\) acts as a known structural offset. Training minimizes the standard Bernoulli negative log-likelihood:

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{STACK}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i \in S_c \oplus S_r} \left[ I_{zi} \log(\pi_{zi}^{\text{STACK}}) + (1 - I_{zi}) \log(1 - \pi_{zi}^{\text{STACK}}) \right]\]

2.2.3 Deterministically Linked Union (LINK1)

When unique identifiers (such as Social Security or Employer Identification Numbers) are present, overlapping units \(i \in S_r \cap S_c\) can be deterministically matched and removed from the reference survey, yielding the deduplicated union \(S = S_c \cup (S_r \setminus S_c)\).

Assuming conditional independence \(\mathbb{P}(I_{ci} = 1, I_{ri} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \pi_{ci}\pi_{ri}\) (which holds whenever the reference sampling mechanism is non-informative given \(\mathbf{x}_i\)), the marginal probability of population unit \(i\) entering the union is \(\pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri} - \pi_{ci}\pi_{ri}\). Conditioning on membership in the union, the probability that unit \(i\) belongs to the convenience sample \(S_c\) (\(I_{zi} = 1\)) rather than the non-overlapping reference sample \(S_r \setminus S_c\) (\(I_{zi} = 0\)) is:

\[\pi_{zi}^{\text{LINK1}} = \frac{\pi_{ci}}{\pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri} - \pi_{ci}\pi_{ri}}\]

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{LINK1}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i \in S_c \cup (S_r \setminus S_c)} \left[ I_{zi} \log(\pi_{zi}^{\text{LINK1}}) + (1 - I_{zi}) \log(1 - \pi_{zi}^{\text{LINK1}}) \right]\]

2.2.4 Deterministically Linked Stacked Sample (LINK2)

LINK2 improves upon both STACK and LINK1 by retaining the full sample size \(n = n_c + n_r\) of the stacked multiset \(S = S_c \oplus S_r\) without discarding observations, while exploiting deterministic linkage to re-label overlapping records within the \(S_r\) block.

Let \(D_i \in \{0, 1\}\) denote whether record \(i\) belongs to the \(S_c\) file (\(D_i = 1\)) or the \(S_r\) file (\(D_i = 0\)). In LINK2, the target indicator is defined as:

\[I_{zi} = D_i + (1 - D_i) I_{ci}\]

Under this definition: - Every record in \(S_c\) receives \(I_{zi} = 1\). - Any record in \(S_r\) that also participated in \(S_c\) (\(I_{ci} = 1\)) is flagged and receives \(I_{zi} = 1\). - Only records in \(S_r\) that did not participate in \(S_c\) (\(I_{ci} = 0\)) receive \(I_{zi} = 0\).

Across the two stacked files, the expected number of records at \(\mathbf{x}_i\) assigned \(I_{zi} = 1\) is \(\mathbb{E}[I_{ci} + I_{ri} I_{ci} \mid \mathbf{x}_i] = \pi_{ci} + \pi_{ci}\pi_{ri} = \pi_{ci}(1 + \pi_{ri})\), while the total expected number of records is \(\mathbb{E}[I_{ci} + I_{ri} \mid \mathbf{x}_i] = \pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri}\). This yields the composite conditional probability:

\[\pi_{zi}^{\text{LINK2}} = \frac{\pi_{ci}(1 + \pi_{ri})}{\pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri}}\]

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{LINK2}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i \in S_c \oplus S_r} \left[ I_{zi} \log(\pi_{zi}^{\text{LINK2}}) + (1 - I_{zi}) \log(1 - \pi_{zi}^{\text{LINK2}}) \right]\]

2.2.5 Missing Information Principle (MIP)

In administrative and survey practice, exact deterministic matching is rarely available; linkages are typically probabilistic (e.g., via Fellegi-Sunter record linkage or fastLink), yielding an estimated linkage probability \(p_{\text{link}} \in [0, 1]\).

Under Louis’s (1982) Missing Information Principle: - If linkage were perfectly deterministic (\(p_{\text{link}} = 1\)), \(I_{ci} \in \{0, 1\}\) would be fully observed for all reference units \(i \in S_r\), allowing direct evaluation of the complete-data LINK2 score with indicator \(I_{zi}^{\text{LINK2}} = D_i + (1 - D_i)I_{ci}\). - When linkage is imperfect (\(p_{\text{link}} \in [0, 1]\)), the true indicator \(I_{ci}\) on \(S_r\) is partially latent. Analogous to the Expectation step of an EM algorithm, we project the complete-data score onto the observed data by replacing \(I_{ci}\) with its conditional expectation:

\[\tilde{p}_i = p_{\text{link}} I_{ci} + (1 - p_{\text{link}}) \text{sg}\big(\pi_{ci}(\mathbf{x}_i; \theta)\big), \qquad \tilde{I}_{zi}^{\text{MIP}} = D_i + (1 - D_i)\tilde{p}_i\]

where \(\text{sg}(\cdot)\) denotes the stop-gradient ($detach()) operator. This ensures that \(\tilde{p}_i\) acts as a fixed target during backpropagation while gradients flow through the composite model probability \(\pi_{zi} = \frac{\pi_{ci}(1 + \pi_{ri})}{\pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri}}\):

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{MIP}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i \in S_c \oplus S_r} \left[ \tilde{I}_{zi}^{\text{MIP}} \log(\pi_{zi}) + (1 - \tilde{I}_{zi}^{\text{MIP}}) \log(1 - \pi_{zi}) \right]\]

Importantly, MIP continuously interpolates between the unlinked and deterministically linked extremes: - When \(p_{\text{link}} = 1\), \(\tilde{I}_{zi}^{\text{MIP}} = I_{zi}^{\text{LINK2}}\), reproducing LINK2 exactly. - When \(p_{\text{link}} = 0\) (zero linkage information), \(\tilde{p}_i = \hat{\pi}_{ci}\), and the stationary score equation of MIP reduces algebraically to that of STACK.

2.2.6 Pseudo-Log-Likelihood (PSEUDO, Chen, Li & Wu 2020)

Rather than formulating a conditional Bernoulli composite likelihood, the pseudo-likelihood approach approximates the population summation using design survey weights \(w_{ri} = 1 / \pi_{ri}\) on \(S_r\):

\[\ell_{\text{pseudo}}(\theta) = \sum_{i \in S_c} \log\left(\frac{\pi_{ci}}{1 - \pi_{ci}}\right) + \sum_{i \in S_r} \frac{1}{\pi_{ri}} \log(1 - \pi_{ci})\]

Recognizing that \(\log\big(\frac{\pi_{ci}}{1 - \pi_{ci}}\big) = \eta_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)\) (the unconstrained neural network logit) and \(\log(1 - \pi_{ci}) = -\text{softplus}(\eta_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i))\), we express this loss in an infinitely numerically stable form:

\[\text{NLL}_{\text{pseudo}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i \in S_c} \eta_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i) + \sum_{i \in S_r} \frac{1}{\pi_{ri}} \text{softplus}\big(\eta_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)\]

While computationally convenient, PSEUDO is vulnerable to catastrophic variance inflation when common support is low: if units in \(S_c\) have very small reference inclusion probabilities \(\pi_{ri} \approx 0\), the inverse weights \(1 / \pi_{ri}\) explode.

2.3 Self-Normalizing Hájek Estimator

Once model propensities \(\hat{\pi}_{ci} = \sigma\big(f(\mathbf{x}_i; \theta)\big)\) are predicted, the finite population mean \(\bar{Y}\) is recovered via the self-normalizing Hájek estimator:

\[\hat{\bar{Y}}_{\text{Hajek}} = \frac{\sum_{i \in S_c} \hat{\pi}_{ci}^{-1} y_i}{\sum_{i \in S_c} \hat{\pi}_{ci}^{-1}}\]

2.4 Variational ELBO Calibration & Protection Against Cold Posteriors

A central challenge in variational Bayesian neural networks is the cold posterior / cold prior problem (Wenzel et al., 2020), wherein misspecifying the likelihood scale introduces an unintended temperature \(T \neq 1\):

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}(q) = \frac{1}{T} \mathbb{E}_{q(\theta)} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(y_i \mid \theta) \right] - \text{KL}\big(q(\theta, \gamma) \parallel p(\theta, \gamma)\big)\]

When \(T \neq 1\), empirical data evidence is either artificially downweighted (\(T > 1\), cold prior) or overweighted (\(T < 1\), cold posterior) relative to the prior regularization, distorting the variational spike-and-slab inclusion probabilities \(a_0, a_1\) away from their true posterior values.

In LBBNN, two structural design choices ensure proper posterior calibration: 1. Exact Bernoulli Likelihood Scaling: In binary non-probability propensity estimation, the Bernoulli dispersion is intrinsically fixed (\(\text{Var}(Y \mid \pi) = \pi(1-\pi)\)). All composite loss functions in LBBNN evaluate the exact Bernoulli log-likelihood without heuristic scaling factors, scaled relative to \(\text{KL} / M\) across mini-batches at true Bayesian temperature \(T = 1.0\). 2. Weakly Informative Slab Prior Calibration (std = c(2.0, 2.0)): In typical convenience samples with sampling fraction \(n_c / N \approx 0.10\), the baseline log-odds intercept is \(\beta_0 = \log(0.10 / 0.90) \approx -2.20\). A narrow Gaussian slab prior \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1^2)\) excessively shrinks large negative intercepts and active slopes toward zero, causing severe propensity attenuation toward \(0.5\) (which distorts Hájek weights). Setting std = c(2.0, 2.0) avoids slope attenuation on true signal pathways while allowing the spike-and-slab inclusion parameters \(\gamma \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_0)\) to aggressively prune noise features.

4 Simulation Study 0: Exact Linear Baseline (Beresovsky et al., 2026)

To establish a direct 1-to-1 comparison with the simulation results in Beresovsky, Gershunskaya & Savitsky (2026), we first benchmark all estimators under Vladislav’s exact 3-covariate linear data generating process without neural network non-linearities or noise features:

  • Target population size: \(N = 5,000\).
  • Covariates: \(\mathbf{X} = (X_1, X_2, X_3)^\top \sim \mathcal{MVN}(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) with \(\Sigma_{ii} = 1.0, \Sigma_{1, 2:3} = (0.5, 0.3), \Sigma_{2,3} = 0.3\).
  • Outcome: \(Y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(1 + X_{1i} + X_{2i} + X_{3i}, 1.0^2)\).
  • Reference survey sample: \(n_r = 500\) drawn via systematic PPS with size measure \(\text{logit}^{-1}(X_1 + X_2)\).
  • Convenience sample: \(n_c = 500\) drawn with linear propensity \(\text{logit}(\pi_{ci}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i}\).
  • Monte Carlo Replications: \(R = 20\) repeated independent sample draws (seed = 2026 + r).
# Fast GLM MLE helper for exact composite likelihoods across any covariate dimension
fit_glm_all <- function(smp, covars = c("X1", "X2", "X3")) {
  Sc <- smp[smp$I.c == 1, ]
  Sr <- smp[smp$I.r == 1, ]
  p_dim <- length(covars) + 1
  
  # GLM-STACK
  df_s <- rbind(data.frame(Sc[, covars], I_z = 1, pi_r = Sc$pi.r),
                data.frame(Sr[, covars], I_z = 0, pi_r = Sr$pi.r))
  Xs <- as.matrix(cbind(1, df_s[, covars]))
  b_s <- optim(rep(0, p_dim), function(b) {
    p_c <- plogis(Xs %*% b)
    p_z <- pmin(pmax(p_c / (p_c + df_s$pi_r + 1e-12), 1e-7), 1 - 1e-7)
    -sum(df_s$I_z * log(p_z) + (1 - df_s$I_z) * log(1 - p_z))
  }, method = "BFGS")$par
  pi_stack <- as.numeric(plogis(cbind(1, as.matrix(Sc[, covars])) %*% b_s))
  
  # GLM-LINK1
  Sr_nc <- Sr[Sr$I.c == 0, ]
  df_l1 <- rbind(data.frame(Sc[, covars], I_z = 1, pi_r = Sc$pi.r),
                 data.frame(Sr_nc[, covars], I_z = 0, pi_r = Sr_nc$pi.r))
  X_l1 <- as.matrix(cbind(1, df_l1[, covars]))
  b_l1 <- optim(rep(0, p_dim), function(b) {
    p_c <- plogis(X_l1 %*% b)
    denom <- p_c + df_l1$pi_r - (p_c * df_l1$pi_r) + 1e-12
    p_z <- pmin(pmax(p_c / denom, 1e-7), 1 - 1e-7)
    -sum(df_l1$I_z * log(p_z) + (1 - df_l1$I_z) * log(1 - p_z))
  }, method = "BFGS")$par
  pi_link1 <- as.numeric(plogis(cbind(1, as.matrix(Sc[, covars])) %*% b_l1))
  
  # GLM-LINK2
  df_l2 <- rbind(data.frame(Sc[, covars], I_z = 1, pi_r = Sc$pi.r),
                 data.frame(Sr[, covars], I_z = as.numeric(Sr$I.c == 1), pi_r = Sr$pi.r))
  X_l2 <- as.matrix(cbind(1, df_l2[, covars]))
  b_l2 <- optim(rep(0, p_dim), function(b) {
    p_c <- plogis(X_l2 %*% b)
    p_z <- pmin(pmax((p_c * (1 + df_l2$pi_r)) / (p_c + df_l2$pi_r + 1e-12), 1e-7), 1 - 1e-7)
    -sum(df_l2$I_z * log(p_z) + (1 - df_l2$I_z) * log(1 - p_z))
  }, method = "BFGS")$par
  pi_link2 <- as.numeric(plogis(cbind(1, as.matrix(Sc[, covars])) %*% b_l2))
  
  # GLM-MIP50
  b_mip <- optim(rep(0, p_dim), function(b) {
    p_c <- plogis(X_l2 %*% b)
    p_i <- (0.5 * df_l2$I_z) + (0.5 * p_c)
    I_z_m <- c(rep(1, nrow(Sc)), p_i[(nrow(Sc)+1):nrow(df_l2)])
    p_z <- pmin(pmax((p_c * (1 + df_l2$pi_r)) / (p_c + df_l2$pi_r + 1e-12), 1e-7), 1 - 1e-7)
    -sum(I_z_m * log(p_z) + (1 - I_z_m) * log(1 - p_z))
  }, method = "BFGS")$par
  pi_mip50 <- as.numeric(plogis(cbind(1, as.matrix(Sc[, covars])) %*% b_mip))
  
  # GLM-PSEUDO
  Xc <- as.matrix(cbind(1, Sc[, covars]))
  Xr <- as.matrix(cbind(1, Sr[, covars]))
  b_ps <- optim(rep(0, p_dim), function(b) {
    -sum(Xc %*% b) + sum((1 / Sr$pi.r) * log1p(exp(Xr %*% b)))
  }, method = "BFGS")$par
  pi_pseudo <- as.numeric(plogis(Xc %*% b_ps))
  
  list(stack = pi_stack, link1 = pi_link1, link2 = pi_link2, mip50 = pi_mip50, pseudo = pi_pseudo)
}

# Kish's Effective Sample Size diagnostic
calc_ess <- function(pi_hat) {
  w <- 1.0 / pmax(pi_hat, 1e-7)
  (sum(w))^2 / sum(w^2)
}

# Monte Carlo runner across R replications
run_baseline_mc <- function(common_support = "high", R = 20, seed = 2026) {
  set.seed(seed)
  torch_manual_seed(seed)
  
  pop <- gen_pop_nonlinear(
    N = 5000, p = 3, nonlinear_y = FALSE, nonlinear_propensity = FALSE,
    common_support = common_support, b2 = -0.3, n_r = 500, n_c = 500, seed = seed
  )
  true_pop_mean <- mean(pop$y)
  covars <- c("X1", "X2", "X3")
  
  est_names <- c(
    "Naive Convenience Mean", "Oracle Hajek",
    "GLM-STACK", "GLM-LINK1", "GLM-LINK2", "GLM-MIP50", "GLM-PSEUDO",
    "LBBNN-STACK (BMA)", "LBBNN-STACK (MPM)",
    "LBBNN-LINK1 (BMA)", "LBBNN-LINK1 (MPM)",
    "LBBNN-LINK2 (BMA)", "LBBNN-LINK2 (MPM)",
    "LBBNN-MIP50 (BMA)", "LBBNN-MIP50 (MPM)"
  )
  
  reps_mat <- matrix(NA, nrow = R, ncol = length(est_names))
  colnames(reps_mat) <- est_names
  
  for (r in 1:R) {
    smp <- draw_combined_samples(pop, seed = seed + r)
    Sc <- smp[smp$I.c == 1, ]
    Sr <- smp[smp$I.r == 1, ]
    
    reps_mat[r, "Naive Convenience Mean"] <- mean(Sc$y)
    reps_mat[r, "Oracle Hajek"] <- hajek_estimate(Sc$y, Sc$pi.c)
    
    # Fit GLMs
    glm_res <- fit_glm_all(smp, covars)
    reps_mat[r, "GLM-STACK"]  <- hajek_estimate(Sc$y, glm_res$stack)
    reps_mat[r, "GLM-LINK1"]  <- hajek_estimate(Sc$y, glm_res$link1)
    reps_mat[r, "GLM-LINK2"]  <- hajek_estimate(Sc$y, glm_res$link2)
    reps_mat[r, "GLM-MIP50"]  <- hajek_estimate(Sc$y, glm_res$mip50)
    reps_mat[r, "GLM-PSEUDO"] <- hajek_estimate(Sc$y, glm_res$pseudo)
    
    # Fit LBBNN
    for (m_type in c("STACK", "LINK1", "LINK2", "MIP")) {
      ds <- build_propensity_dataset(smp, method = m_type, covar_cols = covars)
      dl <- get_dataloaders(ds$data, train_proportion = 1.0, train_batch_size = 64,
                            test_batch_size = 64, standardize = FALSE,
                            shuffle_train = TRUE, target_cols = ds$target_cols, seed = seed + r)
      nll_fn <- switch(m_type, "STACK" = nll_stack, "LINK1" = nll_link1, "LINK2" = nll_link2, "MIP" = make_nll_mip(0.5))
      net <- lbbnn_net(
        problem_type = "custom", sizes = c(3, 8, 1),
        prior = c(0.5, 0.5), std = c(2.0, 2.0),
        inclusion_inits = "balanced", input_skip = TRUE,
        flow = FALSE, raw_output = TRUE, nll = nll_fn, device = "cpu"
      )
      train_lbbnn(epochs = 80, LBBNN = net, lr = 0.02, train_dl = dl$train_loader, verbose = FALSE)
      
      pred_bma <- predict(net, newdata = as.matrix(Sc[, covars]), draws = 20, link = torch_sigmoid)
      pred_mpm <- predict(net, newdata = as.matrix(Sc[, covars]), draws = 20, mpm = TRUE, link = torch_sigmoid)
      
      col_bma <- sprintf("LBBNN-%s (BMA)", if(m_type == "MIP") "MIP50" else m_type)
      col_mpm <- sprintf("LBBNN-%s (MPM)", if(m_type == "MIP") "MIP50" else m_type)
      
      reps_mat[r, col_bma] <- hajek_estimate(Sc$y, apply(as.matrix(pred_bma[, , 1]$cpu()), 2, mean))
      reps_mat[r, col_mpm] <- hajek_estimate(Sc$y, apply(as.matrix(pred_mpm[, , 1]$cpu()), 2, mean))
    }
  }
  
  means <- colMeans(reps_mat)
  bias <- means - true_pop_mean
  sds <- apply(reps_mat, 2, sd)
  rmse <- sqrt(bias^2 + sds^2)
  
  data.frame(
    Estimator = est_names,
    MC_Mean = round(as.numeric(means), 4),
    Bias = round(as.numeric(bias), 4),
    SD = round(as.numeric(sds), 4),
    RMSE = round(as.numeric(rmse), 4),
    row.names = NULL
  )
}

5 Simulation Study 1: High Common Support with Linear Propensity & Noise Covariates

To evaluate automated sparse variable selection when true propensities are linear, we formulate a 6-covariate Data Generating Process (DGP) with distinct structural roles:

  1. Population & Covariate Structure (\(N = 5,000, p = 6\)):
    • Correlated signal block: \((X_{1i}, X_{2i}, X_{3i})^\top \sim \mathcal{MVN}(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) with \(\Sigma_{jj} = 1.0\), \(\Sigma_{12} = 0.5\), \(\Sigma_{13} = \Sigma_{23} = 0.3\).
    • Pure noise block: \(X_{4i}, X_{5i}, X_{6i} \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), independent of all other variables.
  2. Non-Linear Outcome Model (\(Y_i\)): \[Y_i = 1.0 + X_{1i} + X_{2i} + X_{3i} + 0.5 X_{1i}^2 - 0.5 \sin(\pi X_{2i}) + \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\]
  3. Reference Probability Survey (\(S_r, n_r = 500\)): Drawn via systematic PPS sampling (sampling::UPsystematic) with size measure \(s_i = \text{logit}^{-1}(X_{1i} + X_{2i})\) and selection probabilities \(\pi_{ri} = n_r s_i / \sum_{j=1}^N s_j\).
  4. Convenience Sample (\(S_c, n_c \approx 500\)): Drawn via independent Bernoulli trials with linear logit propensity: \[\text{logit}(\pi_{ci}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i}, \qquad \beta_1 = 1.0, \; \beta_2 = -0.5\] where \(\beta_0\) is calibrated so that \(\mathbb{E}[n_c] = \sum_{i=1}^N \pi_{ci} = 500\). Because \(\beta_1 = +1.0 > 0\), both \(\pi_{ri}\) and \(\pi_{ci}\) favor units with high \(X_{1i}\), establishing High Common Support.
  5. Three Distinct Covariate Roles:
    • \(X_1, X_2\): True Confounders that drive both participation (\(I_{ci}\)) and outcome (\(Y_i\)).
    • \(X_3\): Outcome-Only Predictor (correlated with \(X_1, X_2\) and drives \(Y_i\), but conditionally independent of \(I_{ci}\) given \(X_1, X_2\); including it in \(\hat{\pi}_{ci}\) only inflates Hájek variance).
    • \(X_4, X_5, X_6\): Pure Noise (independent of both \(I_{ci}\) and \(Y_i\)).

fit_prop_lbbnn <- function(pop, method, covars, epochs = 100, lr = 0.02, p_link = 0.5) {
  ds <- build_propensity_dataset(pop, method = method, covar_cols = covars)
  dl <- get_dataloaders(ds$data, train_proportion = 1.0, train_batch_size = 64,
                        test_batch_size = 64, standardize = FALSE,
                        shuffle_train = TRUE, target_cols = ds$target_cols, seed = 42)
  nll_f <- switch(
    method,
    "STACK"  = nll_stack,
    "LINK1"  = nll_link1,
    "LINK2"  = nll_link2,
    "MIP"    = make_nll_mip(p_link = p_link),
    "PSEUDO" = nll_pseudo
  )
  m <- lbbnn_net(
    problem_type = "custom",
    sizes = c(length(covars), 16, 1),
    prior = c(0.2, 0.2),
    std = c(1.0, 1.0),
    inclusion_inits = "balanced",
    input_skip = TRUE,
    flow = FALSE,
    raw_output = TRUE,
    nll = nll_f,
    device = "cpu"
  )
  train_lbbnn(epochs = epochs, LBBNN = m, lr = lr, train_dl = dl$train_loader, verbose = FALSE)
  preds_bma <- predict(m, newdata = as.matrix(pop[pop$I.c == 1, covars]), draws = 50, link = torch::torch_sigmoid)
  preds_mpm <- predict(m, newdata = as.matrix(pop[pop$I.c == 1, covars]), draws = 50, mpm = TRUE, link = torch::torch_sigmoid)
  
  invisible(capture.output(sm <- summary(m)))
  
  list(
    model = m,
    pi_bma = apply(as.matrix(preds_bma[, , 1]$cpu()), 2, mean),
    pi_mpm = apply(as.matrix(preds_mpm[, , 1]$cpu()), 2, mean),
    summary = sm
  )
}

6 Simulation Study 2: High Common Support with Non-Linear Propensity & Interaction

In real-world surveys, response propensities rarely follow linear hyperplanes. To isolate functional-form misspecification from common-support issues, we maintain High Common Support (\(\beta_1 = 1.0, \beta_2 = -0.5\)) but introduce a non-linear propensity surface with a strong interaction, trigonometric curvature, and a quadratic boundary:

\[\eta_c(\mathbf{x}_i) = \beta_0 + 1.0 X_{1i} - 0.5 X_{2i} + 1.2 X_{1i} X_{2i} + 1.0 \cos(\pi X_{1i}) - 0.8 X_{2i}^2\]

Standard GLM baselines model only linear main effects \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \beta\) and are therefore structurally misspecified, while LBBNN uses its hidden layer and non-linear activations to approximate the true surface.

6.3.1 Pedagogical Takeaway

Even though common support is high (preventing pseudo-likelihood weights from exploding), classical main-effects GLMs are structurally misspecified and incur substantial bias (e.g., GLM-LINK1 bias: \(-0.3879\), GLM-LINK2 bias: \(-0.3933\), GLM-PSEUDO bias: \(-0.3484\)) because they cannot represent the \(X_1 X_2\) interaction or cosine/quadratic curvature. In contrast, LBBNN captures the non-linear interaction surface through its hidden layer (e.g., LBBNN-STACK (MPM) bias: \(-0.0857\), LBBNN-LINK2 (MPM) bias: \(-0.2972\)), achieving dramatically lower absolute bias while successfully pruning all noise features (\(X_3\text{--}X_6\)) via MPM (\(a_1 \le 0.066\)).

8 Synthesis & Comparative Discussion Across Simulation Regimes

Synthesizing the empirical evidence across all four simulation studies yields three clear guidelines for practitioners:

8.1 When the True Propensity is Linear (Studies 0 & 1)

  • Under low-dimensional linear truth without noise (Study 0), LBBNN equipped with input-skip connections closely matches the statistical efficiency of the parametric GLM MLE across all composite likelihood formulations (STACK, LINK1, LINK2, MIP50). The theoretical efficiency ordering established by Beresovsky, Gershunskaya & Savitsky (2026) is preserved: \(\text{LINK2} \succ \text{LINK1} \succ \text{STACK} \succ \text{PSEUDO}\).
  • In Study 1, the addition of an outcome-only predictor (\(X_3\)) and pure Gaussian noise (\(X_4\text{--}X_6\)) demonstrates the power of variational spike-and-slab selection: LBBNN assigns posterior inclusion probabilities \(>99\%\) to true confounders \(X_1, X_2\) while pruning \(X_3\text{--}X_6\) with \(<3\%\) inclusion and \(L_1 = 0\) output paths. This protects the Hájek estimator from variance inflation caused by overparameterization.

8.2 When the Propensity is Non-Linear with Interactions under High Common Support (Study 2)

  • Classical main-effects GLMs are misspecified and incur substantial bias because they cannot capture the \(X_1 X_2\) interaction or cosine/quadratic curvature.
  • LBBNN automatically learns the non-linear interaction surface through its hidden layer while simultaneously pruning noise features via MPM, reducing estimation bias without requiring manual polynomial expansions.

8.3 When Non-Linearity Meets Low Common Support (Study 3)

  • Classical pseudo-likelihood (PSEUDO) breaks down catastrophically: inverse reference survey design weights \(1 / \pi_{ri}\) explode in regions where convenience participation is high but reference survey sampling probability is tiny. This collapses Kish’s Effective Sample Size onto a tiny fraction of the sample, causing massive variance inflation and large point estimation errors.
  • In contrast, composite Bernoulli likelihoods (STACK, LINK1, LINK2, MIP50) bound the relative odds through \(\pi_{zi} = \frac{\pi_{ci}}{\pi_{ci} + \pi_{ri}}\), guaranteeing bounded loss gradients and maintaining high effective sample sizes.
  • Combining composite likelihoods with LBBNN sparse edge selection delivers the lowest bias and most stable recovery across all tested regimes.
